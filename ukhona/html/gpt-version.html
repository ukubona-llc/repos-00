I
Foundation · Physics · Equal Temperament

A440 & THE
FRACTAL FORMULA

The 12th root of 2. The Pythagorean comma. Why Bach wept. And who engineered the cage that set music free.

Modern Western music is built on one agreed-upon lie: no interval is pure. In exchange for this lie, every key is equally impure — and therefore equally usable. This is 12-tone equal temperament (12-TET), and it is the foundation of every piano, every pop song, every symphony written after roughly 1700.

The Formula — All 88 Notes from One f(n) = 440 × 2^(n/12)

440 Hz = A4 · n = semitone distance from A4 · 2 = octave doubling
Multiply by ¹²√2 ≈ 1.05946 to ascend one semitone.
Multiply by 2 to ascend one octave. The fractal repeats infinitely.

The alternative — the harmonic series — gives us acoustically pure intervals: a perfect fifth at exactly 3:2, a major third at 5:4. Beautiful. But it accumulates error as you modulate. After 12 perfect fifths, you're slightly sharp of where you started — the infamous Pythagorean comma (1.01364…). Some keys sound divine. Others sound broken. Equal temperament distributes this error evenly across all 12 keys. No key sounds perfect. No key sounds wrong.

"Temperament sacrifices purity for symmetry. Every key gets the same slight imperfection. It is a democratic lie — and music has never been the same."

The architects of this shift:

Without equal temperament: no Chopin. No Stevie. No jazz modulation. No anything that moves between distant keys. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier is not a pedagogical exercise — it is a manifesto. One prelude and fugue in every major and minor key: it all works. See?

THE SEVEN
DIATONIC 7TH CHORDS

Not a warm-up. Not a beginner exercise. This is what you do every morning until you die.

In any major key, stacking thirds above each scale degree produces seven seventh chords — one for each degree. Together they form the complete harmonic skeleton of Bach, Stevie Wonder, jazz, R&B, and film scoring. You do not graduate from these. You go deeper.

III
Case Study · Stevie Wonder · Melody as Seventh

"LATELY" —
THE 7TH AS HOME

Adult harmony disguised as pop. The melody doesn't visit the 7th — it lives there.

"Lately" (1980) is perhaps the most elegant proof that the 7th degree of a chord can function not as tension but as arrival. Most melodies orbit the root, third, and fifth — the safe tones. Stevie plants his melody on the 7th, again and again, and makes it feel like the most natural resting place in the world.

Harmonic Architecture — "Lately" (Eb major, simplified) Ebmaj7 — Fm7 — Bb7 — Gm7 — Cm7 — Fm7 — Bb7 Walk-up bass: Eb — F — G — Ab — Bb Melody peak notes: D (maj7 of Ebmaj7) Eb (7th in Fm7 context) Ab (7th of Bb7) The 7th is not the exception. The 7th IS the melody.

The walk-up bass line is the mechanism: a scalar ascent in the bass forces each melody note into a new harmonic context. A note that was the root a beat ago is suddenly the 7th of a passing chord. Stevie doesn't resolve it — he holds it, or moves further up. This is compositional courage masquerading as simplicity.

"Play the chord. Sing only the 7th. Hear how it aches. That ache IS the lesson."

The deeper observation: "Lately" also contains borrowed chords — specifically the iv minor (Ab minor in Eb major), importing a Cb from the parallel minor key. This is the gateway to Module IV. Stevie uses modal interchange not as a theoretical device but as an emotional one: the borrowed chord arrives and the 7th above it is suddenly even more tender.

Stevie Wonder is the modern Bach. Not metaphorically — structurally. He organizes melody, harmony, and voice leading with the same contrapuntal intelligence, disguised in contemporary feel.

IV
Borrowed Chords · Parallel Minor · Color Theory

MODAL
INTERCHANGE

Emotional weather systems crossing key boundaries. You are still home — you just opened the door to the basement.

Modal interchange is the act of importing a chord from a parallel mode (same root, different scale structure) into the home key. The most common source: the parallel minor. A chord that doesn't belong to C major — say, Fm (F–Ab–C) — is heard not as a modulation but as a deepening, a shadow cast from the minor world into the major one without changing our address.

"Modal interchange doesn't change the key. It expands the emotional range of the key. You open a door to a parallel world and let some of its darkness in."

In "Lately," Stevie uses the iv minor (Ab minor in Eb) to devastating effect. The Ab — the flatted sixth of Eb major — arrives from the parallel minor and transforms the mood without losing the tonal center. This is the most powerful technique in popular harmony: darkness within light, minor within major, borrowed grief inside a major-key song.

Listen for this everywhere once you hear it: Beatles. Radiohead. Beyoncé. Every great ballad uses it. Every film composer lives here. And Spanish Phrygian is essentially the full surrender to this borrowing — which brings us to Module V.

V
Seven Modes · Spanish Phrygian · Altered Dominant · Two Vocabularies

THE MODES &
PHRYGIAN'S SHADOW

What flamenco calls a tonic, jazz calls an altered dominant. Same pitches. Different gravity.

The seven modes are rotations of the major scale — each beginning on a different degree, each carrying a distinct emotional world. The same 7 notes; 7 different ways to feel about them depending on which one you call home.

ION · DOR · PHR · LYD · MIX · AEO · LOC

The Spanish Phrygian (Phrygian Dominant, 5th mode of harmonic minor) is Phrygian with a raised major third: 1, ♭2, 3, 4, 5, ♭6, ♭7. That interval between ♭2 and the major 3rd is an augmented second — the sound of flamenco, of the Middle East, of ancient ceremony. In E: E — F — G# — A — B — C — D.

Two Vocabularies — Same Sound FOLK / FLAMENCO NAME: Spanish Phrygian / Phrygian Dominant Cadence: ♭II → I (F major → E major) Heard as: tonic with tension above it BERKLEE / JAZZ NAME: Mixolydian ♭9, ♯9, ♭13 (Altered Dominant / V7alt) Voicing: G — B — D♭(♭9) — E♭(♯9) — F — E♭(♭13) Heard as: dominant resolving to tonic (C or Cm) Same scale. Different tonal gravity. Context determines meaning.

The ♯9 chord — dominant 7th with sharpened ninth — became the signature of 1960s psychedelic and funk (the "Hendrix chord," E7♯9). The ♭9 adds menace to the dominant over minor resolutions. The ♭13 creates ambiguity between major and minor. Together: the altered scale, or as musicians call it, that sound.

"What flamenco hears as a tonic in repose, jazz hears as a dominant in maximum tension. Both are right. Both are using the same seven notes."
VI
Bryan Adams · Phrygian for Western Ears · It's Always the Canadians

"HAVE YOU REALLY
EVER LOVED A WOMAN"

The most perfectly calibrated deployment of Spanish Phrygian in mainstream Western pop. F major → E major. That's it. That's the whole trick.

Bryan Adams, 1995, from Don Juan DeMarco. Orchestrated by Michael Kamen. Nylon-string guitar. This song exists to demonstrate that the ♭II → I cadence (F major → E major in E Phrygian) is the most romantically effective two-chord move available to a Western ear that doesn't live in the flamenco tradition.

Phrygian Harmonic Gesture — Key of E Guitar descending line: E — D — C — B Then: F major arrives (the ♭II chord) Then: resolves back to E major (the I) The F chord is the Neapolitan — the defining sound of Phrygian harmony. It doesn't resolve tension. It IS the tension. You arrive at E not relieved — but aching.

What makes this work for Western ears is the context loading: nylon string = flamenco = passion = Spain = warmth. The ♭II chord doesn't need explanation. It is felt as a musical exhale — a longing that has no clean resolution because longing never does. The melody over the F chord sits on notes from E Phrygian — the ♭2 (F) and ♭6 (C) — the two most "non-Western" tones, deployed at maximum emotional exposure.

"The Canadians do this well. Cohen. Mitchell. Céline. And here — Bryan Adams bringing a 500-year-old Andalusian cadence to a pop soundtrack and making it feel like the truest expression of love."

Ears-first exercise: play F major on a piano. Then E major. Just those two chords, back and forth. Feel the pull. Five centuries of Andalusian music compressed into two chords. You now understand the entire Phrygian tradition. Scale up from there.

VII
Tchaikovsky · Pathétique · Half-Diminished as First Word

THE vii∅7
THAT OPENS EVERYTHING

The most unstable chord in the diatonic system. Opening the final movement of his last symphony. Before his death. I shit you not.

Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74. Pathétique. Premiered October 28, 1893. Tchaikovsky died November 6. The fourth movement — Adagio lamentoso — is one of the most psychologically honest pieces of music ever written. It ends with a B minor chord that simply fades into silence. But we are concerned with how it begins.

Opening Chord — 4th Movement, Pathétique Chord: A# — C# — E — G# Analysis: vii∅7 in B minor (Half-diminished 7th on the leading tone) Contains a tritone (A#–E). Has no stable fifth above the root. Desperately wants to resolve. Tchaikovsky opens with this chord. He was telling you, from the first breath: this will not resolve the way you expect.

Every convention of 1893 orchestral writing demands you open a finale with tonic stability — a B minor chord, a dominant F#, something that orients the listener. Tchaikovsky opens with the most harmonically incomplete chord in the system. The chord that exists only in relation to where it's going. The chord that has no home.

"To begin with vii∅7 is to say: we are already in the grief. There is no setup. The pain existed before the first note."

This is the full synthesis of the curriculum. The formula gave us 88 equal notes (I). The diatonic 7ths gave us the vii∅7 as a household sound (II). Stevie showed us the 7th can be rest rather than tension (III). Modal interchange showed us darkness inside light (IV). Phrygian showed us the ♭II's ache (V–VI). And Tchaikovsky — dying, nine days from the end — showed us that the most unstable chord in the system can be the first thing said. Can be the truest thing said.

Teach students: play vii∅7 resolving to I. Then delay the resolution. Then delay it more. Hear the dread accumulate. That dread is the half-diminished chord doing its only job: waiting.

Tchaikovsky didn't wait. He put it first.
