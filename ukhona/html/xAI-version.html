I
Foundation · Physics · History

The Foundation of Pitch —
A440 and the Piano's 88 Notes

Objective: Understand the standard tuning system, how it creates the piano's notes, and why it liberated composers.

A440 — the note A above middle C vibrating at 440 Hz — is the international standard pitch around which all other notes are built. From this single anchor, every one of the piano's 88 keys is derived using a single formula:

f = 440 × 2n/12

Where n is the number of semitones from A4. This is a geometric progression — self-similar, fractal — each octave doubling frequency, each semitone multiplying by the 12th root of 2 (≈ 1.05946). The same ratio, repeated infinitely in both directions.

  • The harmonic series (natural overtones: octave 1:2, fifth 2:3) gives pure, beatless intervals — but accumulates errors across keys. Modulating to a distant key in just intonation sounds broken.
  • Equal temperament distributes this error evenly: no interval is perfectly pure, but every key is equally usable. A democratic compromise.
  • Before this system, keyboards favored certain keys. J.S. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (1722) was a manifesto demonstrating all 24 major and minor keys as equally viable.
  • The mathematical foundation was independently developed by Zhu Zaiyu (China, 1584) and Simon Stevin (Flemish engineer, 1585). Hermann von Helmholtz later formalized the acoustic science.

The constraint that equal temperament imposes — sacrificing purity for symmetry — is what makes all of modern music possible. Without it: no Chopin, no Stevie Wonder, no jazz harmony, no global modulation.

Daily Practice · Harmony · All 12 Keys

Daily Diatonic 7th Chords —
Building Blocks in Every Key

Objective: Master the seven diatonic 7th chords as lifelong finger and ear training.

In any major key, stacking thirds above each scale degree produces seven distinct seventh chords — the complete harmonic skeleton of all tonal music. These are not a beginner exercise. They are daily practice forever: the deeper you go, the more you hear.

I maj7
ii min7
iii min7
IV maj7
V7 ★
vi min7
vii∅7 ◈
  • In C major: I maj7 (C-E-G-B) · ii min7 (D-F-A-C) · iii min7 (E-G-B-D) · IV maj7 (F-A-C-E) · V7 dom (G-B-D-F) · vi min7 (A-C-E-G) · vii∅7 half-dim (B-D-F-A♭)
  • The V7 dominant seventh contains a tritone (3rd and 7th) that resolves almost gravitationally to the tonic — the engine of all Western harmonic motion.
  • The vii∅7 half-diminished is the most unstable chord in the system. It is also the most emotionally potent. More on this in Module VII.
  • Practice in all 12 keys, cycling through the circle of fifths: C → G → D → A → E → B → F# → D♭ → A♭ → E♭ → B♭ → F

This trains harmony, voice leading, and key fluency simultaneously. Practice block chords, arpeggios, and smooth inversions. The goal is not speed — it is to hear each chord's character in your body.

Activities by Age Group
Toddlers

Clap to "happy" (major) vs "sad" (minor) chord sounds. Bounce to the rhythm.

Kids

Build chords with colored blocks or apps. Sing along to pop songs using these chords.

Teens / Adults

Arpeggiate all seven on piano or guitar. Transpose to random keys without looking.

Seniors

Play simplified two-hand versions. Reflect on which chords appear in favorite songs.

Daily PracticePlay and sing all seven 7th chords in one key per day. Rotate through keys weekly. Add inversions for variety.
III
Case Study · Stevie Wonder · Melody · 7th as Seat

Stevie Wonder's "Lately" —
The 7th as Emotional Seat

Objective: Analyze how melodies "sit" on chord sevenths for poignant, unresolved emotional depth.

"Lately" (1980) is a perfect case study in what happens when a melody chooses to live on the 7th rather than resolve away from it. In D♭ major, the verse follows a I–vi–ii–V pattern with repeating ii–V motion for sustained tension.

  • Over A♭7, the melody frequently lands on G♭ — the ♭7th of the chord — creating a "yearning" quality that the ear expects to resolve but Stevie refuses to release.
  • The walk-up bass line adds layered tension: as the bass ascends, each melody note is recontextualized as the 7th of a new passing chord. The effect is cumulative ache.
  • Most pop melodies gravitate to root, third, fifth — the "safe" tones. Stevie plants the melody on the 7th as a point of arrival. This is compositional courage.
  • Stevie Wonder = the Modern Bach. Same contrapuntal intelligence, same mastery of voice leading — disguised as a pop ballad.

The 7th doesn't "want" to stay — theoretically. But emotionally it can become the most beautiful resting point in the phrase. That tension between what the theory says should happen and what the feeling says is happening is where great melody lives.

Practice instruction: Play the chord. Sing only the 7th of that chord. Sustain it. Hear how it aches. Then resolve it down by step. Feel the difference. The ache before resolution — that IS the lesson.

Activities by Age Group
Toddlers

Sway to the song. Mimic the high, reaching notes with voices.

Kids

Draw the emotions from the melody. Play the simple melody on bells or xylophone.

Teens / Adults

Transcribe the melody by ear. Circle every note that is a 7th of its chord. Count them.

Seniors

Sing along. Share stories of similar ballads from your era that use the same emotional palette.

IV
Borrowed Chords · Parallel Modes · Emotional Color

Modal Interchange —
Borrowing Chords for Richness

Objective: Explore borrowing chords from parallel modes to add emotional color and narrative depth.

Modal interchange is the technique of temporarily importing a chord from a parallel mode (same root, different scale) into the home key. You don't modulate — you reach into a neighboring emotional world and bring one chord back with you.

  • In "Lately": the IV chord (G♭ major) shifts to iv minor (G♭ minor), darkening the harmony for heightened emotional effect. The A♭♭ (flattened 6th of D♭ major) enters from the parallel minor world.
  • Common borrowed chords (in a major key): iv minor (most heartbreaking), ♭VII major (rock grandeur), ♭VI major (cinematic expansion), ♭III major (dark strength).
  • Think of it as: "emotional weather systems crossing key boundaries." You are still at home — you just opened the basement door.
  • Appears widely: Beatles' "In My Life" (IV to iv), virtually every Beyoncé ballad, all of Radiohead's harmonic language, film scoring, jazz standards.

The borrowed chord lands with a recognition that is not rational — your nervous system registers the emotional complexity before your brain processes the theory. This is the composer reaching into two worlds simultaneously, and the listener feeling it without knowing why.

Activities by Age Group
Toddlers

Switch between "bright" and "shadowy" sounds. Describe the feeling with faces and gestures.

Kids

Rewrite a nursery rhyme with one borrowed chord. Hear how the mood shifts.

Teens / Adults

Analyze songs with modal interchange. Compose a 8-bar piece using at least one borrowed chord.

Seniors

Identify borrowed chords in jazz standards and old favorites. Discuss the emotional effect.

♩
Daily PracticeAdd one borrowed chord (try iv minor first) to your daily 7th chord practice. Feel the shift.
V
The Seven Modes · Spanish Phrygian · R&B · Jazz Altered Dominant

The Modes & Spanish Phrygian —
Two Vocabularies, One Sound

Objective: Learn the seven modes; spotlight Spanish Phrygian and its R&B/jazz equivalents in Berklee vocabulary.

The seven modes are rotations of the major scale — same seven notes, seven different emotional worlds depending on which note you call home:

ModeDegreeCharacter
IonianIMajor. Bright, resolved, familiar.
DorianIIMinor + raised 6th. Cool, sophisticated.
PhrygianIIIMinor + ♭2. Severe, ancient, Spanish.
LydianIVMajor + ♯4. Dreamy, floating, film-score.
MixolydianVMajor + ♭7. Blues, rock, soul.
AeolianVINatural minor. Melancholic, folk.
LocrianVIIDiminished tonic. Unstable, angular.
  • Spanish Phrygian (Phrygian Dominant): 1, ♭2, 3, 4, 5, ♭6, ♭7. Phrygian with a raised major third. The interval between ♭2 and 3 is an augmented second — the sound of flamenco and the Middle East.
  • In R&B/jazz (Berklee vocab): the same sound is called Mixolydian ♭9, ♯9, ♭13 — an altered dominant. Used over V7 chords resolving to minor for maximum soulful tension.
  • The ♯9 chord (dominant 7th + sharpened ninth) is the "Hendrix chord" — E7♯9, the signature of 1960s psychedelic funk. The ♭9 adds menace. The ♭13 blurs major and minor.
  • Key insight: flamenco hears this as a tonic at rest. Jazz hears it as a dominant under maximum tension. Same pitches. Context determines meaning.

Teens / Adults

Seniors

VI
Bryan Adams · Western Phrygian · The ♭II Cadence · It's Always the Canadians

Phrygian in Pop —
"Have You Ever Really Loved a Woman"

Objective: Appreciate how Phrygian elements create romantic exoticism for Western ears without overwhelming them.

Bryan Adams, 1995, co-written with Mutt Lange and orchestrated by Michael Kamen — from Don Juan DeMarco. Nylon-string guitar, full orchestra, E Phrygian (E F G# A B C D). This is the most perfectly calibrated deployment of Spanish Phrygian in mainstream Western pop.

  • The defining gesture: F major → E major. The ♭II resolving to I. This is the Phrygian (Neapolitan) cadence. It creates exotic tension without straying so far that a Western ear feels lost.
  • The guitar descends: E — D — C — B, then F major arrives and resolves back to E. The ♭2 (F) and ♭6 (C) — the two most "non-Western" tones — appear at moments of maximum emotional exposure.
  • The texture does crucial work: nylon string = flamenco = passion = Spain. The listener's cultural associations pre-load the emotional meaning before the first chord resolves.
  • "It's always the Canadians" — Cohen, Mitchell, Céline Dion, Adams — bringing centuries-old musical traditions into accessible, warmly human pop contexts.

Ears-first exercise: play only F major, then E major. Repeat. No theory needed — just feel the motion. That is 500 years of Andalusian tradition compressed into two chords. Now you understand the entire Phrygian tradition. Scale up from there.

Note: Grok placed this song in the Spirit: Stallion of the Cimarron soundtrack (2001). The original is from Don Juan DeMarco (1994/95). The Phrygian analysis applies equally to both contexts — the harmonic language is identical.

A small factual note: the original context of the song is Don Juan DeMarco (1995), not Spirit (2001), though Bryan Adams did contribute to that soundtrack as well.

VII
Tchaikovsky · Pathétique · vii∅7 · Classical Drama · Existential Instability

Classical Drama —
Tchaikovsky's Finale and the vii∅7 Opening

Objective: Examine how the half-diminished chord creates unbearable tension — and what it means to open a symphony with it.

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74 — the Pathétique, premiered nine days before his death in 1893 — opens its fourth movement (Adagio lamentoso) not with stability, not with a tonic chord, but with the most harmonically incomplete chord in the diatonic system.

vii∅7 in B minor: A# — C# — E — G#
  • The half-diminished seventh chord (vii∅7): built on the leading tone, containing a diminished fifth and a tritone. No stable fifth above the root. It exists only in relation to where it's going — it has no home of its own.
  • Every convention of 1893 orchestral writing demands an opening chord of tonic stability. Tchaikovsky opens with the most unstable chord available. He was telling you: we are already in the grief.
  • Half-diminished harmony in practice: play vii∅7, then resolve to I. Then delay the resolution. Then delay it further. The dread accumulates. That accumulation is the chord's entire emotional life.
  • In jazz: this same chord is the ii∅7 in a minor ii–V–i (e.g., Bø7 → E7alt → Am). The "Portal" chord. It opens the door; the altered dominant walks through it.

The full curriculum synthesis: A440 gave us equal temperament (I). The diatonic 7ths gave us vii∅7 as a household sound (II). Stevie showed the 7th as rest, not tension (III). Modal interchange showed darkness inside light (IV). Phrygian showed the ♭II's ache (V–VI). And Tchaikovsky — nine days from death — showed that the most unstable chord in the system can be the first word, and the truest one.

The symphony ends with a quiet B minor chord that simply stops. No triumph. No resolution. Just: silence. Between the opening vii∅7 and that final silence, everything human that music can express.

Activities by Age Group
Toddlers

React to "spooky," tense chords with faces and body language. Describe the feeling.

Kids

Storytime with symphony excerpts. Draw what happens in the story the music tells.

Teens / Adults

Orchestral score analysis. Compose an ending using vii∅7 → V7 → i. Delay the resolution as long as possible.

Seniors

Listen to the full 4th movement. Discuss: what does it feel like to hear a piece that knows it's ending?
