Module I

A440, the Twelfth Root of Two,
& Why Bach Wept with Relief

Equal Temperament · The Piano's 88 Notes · The Harmonic Series · Andreas Werckmeister & Hermann von Helmholtz

Every note on a modern piano descends from a single, agreed-upon fact: A4 = 440 Hz. Concert pitch. The anchor. From this one stake in the ground, we derive all 87 remaining notes using a formula so elegant it borders on the miraculous — and so impure it scandalized a generation of Renaissance mathematicians.

fn = 440 × (²¹²√2)n

where n = number of semitones above (positive) or below (negative) A4
Each octave doubles the frequency. Each semitone multiplies by the 12th root of 2 ≈ 1.05946…

That number — the 12th root of 2 — is the cornerstone of all Western music. It is irrational, meaning it never terminates or repeats. And here is the profound philosophical move our ancestors made: they accepted an infinite decimal as the price of modulatory freedom.

Before this bargain, tuning systems obeyed the harmonic series — the physics of a vibrating string, where intervals like the perfect fifth (3:2) and major third (5:4) are pure, beatless, gorgeous. Just intonation and its cousins produced heavenly intervals in a single key. The problem? Modulate to a distant key — say, from C major to F# major — and the accumulated tuning errors made the intervals sound like a poorly maintained harpsichord in a cellar. Keyboards tuned in just intonation weren't so much instruments as they were instruments for one key only.

The Mathematician Behind the Freedom

The German organist and theorist Andreas Werckmeister (1645–1706) is the unsung hero here. His 1691 treatise Musicalische Temperatur proposed "well temperament" — not yet equal temperament, but a system of distributed compromises that made all 24 keys playable, each with its own subtle color. J.S. Bach wrote the Well-Tempered Clavier (Books I & II, 1722 and 1742) explicitly as a demonstration of this freedom: 24 preludes and fugues, one in every major and minor key. It was a manifesto.

True 12-tone equal temperament was later rigorously formalized acoustically by Hermann von Helmholtz in On the Sensations of Tone (1863), and became the universal standard through the 19th and early 20th centuries, standardized internationally at A=440 Hz in 1939.

The trade-off is this: in equal temperament, no interval except the octave is acoustically pure. Every fifth is 2 cents flat of just. Every major third is 14 cents sharp. But every key is equally usable, equally impure, equally free. It is a democratic compromise — every key gets the same slight imperfection.

It is, structurally, a fractal: the same ratio applied repeatedly, wrapping around itself every 12 steps, every 12 semitones an octave, every octave doubling the frequency. You can begin at any point and the pattern holds. Bach knew this intuitively. Werckmeister gave him the key. Helmholtz explained why it worked. And your piano is the living proof.

Toddlers (2–5)

Sing an A. Feel that it matches the piano. Sing higher — that's an octave!

Kids (6–12)

Count the 12 keys in an octave. Why 12? Because the math works out perfectly.

Teens

Calculate f for middle C (n = −9 from A4). Verify on a tuner app.

Adults & Seniors

Listen to a just-tuned major chord vs. equal-tempered. Hear the difference — then hear why Bach chose the latter.

Module II

The Seven Diatonic 7th Chords:
Daily Practice. Forever.

Harmonic Language · All 12 Keys · Muscle Memory as Enlightenment

If there is one non-negotiable daily ritual in music — more important than scales, more revealing than arpeggios — it is cycling through the seven diatonic seventh chords in every key. This is not a beginner exercise you graduate from. It is what you do every morning until you die, and every morning it teaches you something new.

In any major key, every degree of the scale generates a naturally occurring seventh chord when you stack thirds above it. Each has a distinct quality, a distinct emotional character, a distinct function. Together they are the complete harmonic vocabulary of Western tonal music.

Scale Degree Roman Numeral Quality In C Major Character / Function
1st I maj7 Major 7th C–E–G–B Home. Lush, arrived, serene.
2nd ii min7 Minor 7th D–F–A–C Pre-dominant. Yearning, soft tension.
3rd iii min7 Minor 7th E–G–B–D Ambiguous. Can substitute for I or V.
4th IV maj7 Major 7th F–A–C–E Subdominant. Bright, open, floating.
5th V7 Dominant 7th G–B–D–F The engine. Maximum tension → resolution.
6th vi min7 Minor 7th A–C–E–G Relative minor. Melancholic, relatable.
7th vii∅7 Half-Diminished B–D–F–A The dangerous one. See Module VII.

The Practice Protocol

Play all seven chords in root position, then in all inversions, then voice-led smoothly from one to the next. Then move to the next key (around the circle of fifths: C → G → D → A → E → B → F# → Db → Ab → Eb → Bb → F). Slow tempo. Hear every note. This is not a warm-up. This is the practice.

At tempo, the whole sequence in 12 keys takes under 10 minutes. Over a lifetime, it is the most efficient investment in musical intelligence a person can make.

Why seventh chords and not triads? Because the seventh is where harmony gets honest. A triad is a photograph; a seventh chord is a face. The added note introduces color, ambiguity, and movement. It's what tells you whether a chord wants to stay or go, whether it's nostalgic or hopeful, whether it's inside the key or reaching outside it.

The dominant seventh (V7) deserves special reverence. It is the only chord in the diatonic system that contains a tritone — the interval between the 3rd and 7th of the chord (in G7: B and F, six semitones apart, the most dissonant diatonic interval). This tritone resolves almost gravitationally: B moves up to C, F moves down to E. The V7→I resolution is the fundamental engine of all Western tonal music. Everything else is context around that one move.

Module III

Stevie Wonder's "Lately":
The Seventh as Home

Melody · The Major 7th Interval · Walk-Up Bass Lines · Case Study in Harmonic Sophistication

"Lately" (1980) is one of the most perfectly constructed pop songs in existence. It is also, quietly, a masterclass in how a melody can live on the seventh — the most harmonically charged note in any chord — and make it feel like the most natural place in the world to breathe.

Most pop melodies gravitate toward the root, the third, or the fifth of the underlying chord — the "safe" tones, the chord tones that ring clearly without friction. Stevie does something braver: he sets the melody, again and again, on the seventh. Not as an accidental, not as a passing tone — as the point of arrival. The melody lands there and rests.

Harmonic / Melodic Architecture — "Lately" Key: Eb major (concert) → modulates through borrowed chords

Intro/Verse chords (simplified): Ebmaj7 — Fm7 — Bb7 — Gm7 — Cm7 — Fm7 — Bb7

Melody peak notes frequently: D (the maj7 of Ebmaj7), Eb (the 7th of Fm7 context),
Ab (the 7th of Bb7) — the seventh is not the exception; it IS the melody.

Walk-up bass line in pre-chorus: Eb — F — G — Ab — Bb
Each step raises tension. The melody above it ascends in parallel tension.
The 7th of each passing chord is where Stevie's voice dwells.
"The walk-up is not about the bass. It's about the notes the melody is forced into — and Stevie chooses to stay on the 7th every time he could resolve. That's courage in songwriting."

The walk-up bass line in "Lately" is a textbook case of a technique that creates irresistible forward motion: a scalar ascent in the bass while the harmony shifts beneath a sustained or rising melody. Each new bass note implies a new chord, often a passing chord or a borrowed chord, and the melody note that was "safe" a beat ago suddenly becomes the seventh of the new harmony. Rather than resolving it, Stevie holds on — or moves up to the next seventh.

This is also where students first encounter non-chord tones that feel better than resolution. The conventional teaching is that sevenths need to resolve down by step. Stevie's teaching is: only if you want to. Sometimes the seventh is the most beautiful place to stay.

Practice Instruction

Transcribe the melody of "Lately" by ear. Write the chord underneath each note. Circle every note that is a 7th of its chord. Count them. You will be astonished. Then: try to sing those same melody notes over a simpler, less sophisticated chord progression. Hear how much the harmonic context does. The notes are the same; the meaning is entirely different.

Young Beginners

Sing along. Notice the sad-beautiful feeling. That's the 7th doing its work.

Intermediate

Play the chord progression. Identify each seventh chord quality.

Advanced

Transcribe the inner voice movement. Find where Modal Interchange enters (→ Module IV).

Module IV

Modal Interchange:
Borrowing from the Dark

Parallel Modes · The Borrowed Chord · Emotional Depth · "Lately" Revisited

Modal interchange — also called borrowed chords — is the technique of temporarily importing a chord from a parallel mode (same root, different scale structure) into the home key. It is one of the most emotionally potent tools in tonal harmony, and once you hear it, you cannot stop hearing it everywhere.

The most common borrowing is from the parallel minor. If you are in C major and you play a Fm chord (F–Ab–C), that Ab does not belong to C major. It comes from C minor (or more specifically, C Aeolian). But it sounds like it belongs — because our ears recognize the root (F) and accept the borrowed color (Ab) as a deepening of the harmony rather than a modulation to a new key.

Most Common Borrowed Chords in Major Keys (borrowed from parallel minor / Dorian / Phrygian)

♭VII major (e.g., Bb major in C) — from C Mixolydian / C Aeolian. The "rock borrowing." Sounds powerful, unresolved.

iv minor (e.g., Fm in C major) — from C Aeolian. Heartbreaking. The most used borrowed chord in all of R&B and soul.

♭VI major (e.g., Ab major in C) — from C Aeolian. Cinematic, expansive. Feels like a key change but isn't.

♭III major (e.g., Eb major in C) — from C Aeolian. Dark grandeur. Common in film scores.

ii∅ / ii°7 — from Phrygian / Locrian. See Module V and VII.

Return now to "Lately." Listen specifically for the moment when the harmony takes a darker, more aching turn — where Stevie moves to a chord that doesn't strictly belong in Eb major. The iv minor chord (Ab minor in Eb) appears with devastating effect: it introduces a Cb (the flattened sixth of Eb major, from Eb minor) into a progression that was otherwise in the major key. The melody over this chord — still sitting on a seventh — is suddenly the seventh of a minor seventh chord. Same technique, doubled emotional weight.

Modal interchange is why certain pop songs make you feel something you can't name. You're in a major key — which signals happiness, stability — but suddenly there's a chord from the minor parallel, and your nervous system registers the emotional complexity before your brain can process the theory. This is the composer reaching into two emotional worlds simultaneously.

"The borrowed chord doesn't change the key. It expands the emotional range of the key. You are still home — you just opened a door to the basement."

Beyond "Lately," listen for this in: Beatles' "Borrowed Time" (♭VII everywhere), Radiohead's entire harmonic language, virtually every Beyoncé ballad, and — as we'll see — the heart of what makes the Spanish Phrygian so arresting.

Module V

The Modes, the Spanish Phrygian,
& R&B's Secret Weapon

The Seven Modes · Phrygian Dominant · Mixolydian ♭9 ♯9 ♭13 · Berklee Vocabulary

The seven modes are rotations of the major scale — each starting on a different degree, each with a different interval pattern and a different emotional world:

ModeBuilt on degreePattern (W=whole, H=half)Emotional Character
IonianIW W H W W W HMajor. Bright, resolved.
DorianIIW H W W W H WMinor with raised 6th. Cool, sophisticated.
PhrygianIIIH W W W H W WMinor with ♭2. Severe, ancient, Spanish.
LydianIVW W W H W W HMajor with ♯4. Dreamy, floating, film-score.
MixolydianVW W H W W H WMajor with ♭7. Blues, rock, soul.
AeolianVIW H W W H W WNatural minor. Melancholic, folk.
LocrianVIIH W W H W W WDiminished tonic. Unstable, angular.

Now: the Spanish Phrygian (also called Phrygian Dominant or the fifth mode of harmonic minor) is not strictly a diatonic mode — it is Phrygian with a raised third. Its formula: H–W+H–H–H–W+H–W (1, ♭2, 3, 4, 5, ♭6, ♭7). That raised major third over a Phrygian foundation creates an augmented second — the interval from ♭2 to 3 — which is the hallmark sound of flamenco, Middle Eastern music, and one of the most viscerally arresting sounds the Western ear encounters.

Phrygian Dominant in Practice (E as root)

E — F — G# — A — B — C — D — E

The E major chord (E–G#–B) sits over a scale that begins with F (the ♭2). The chord resolving TO this E major from the F chord above it — that is the Spanish Phrygian cadence. ♭II → I. Hear it in flamenco, in Bond themes, in every tense film scene where the villain is about to do something memorable.

Now, in R&B and funk contexts, the Berklee vocabulary names this same harmonic zone differently — because the approach is from the dominant side rather than the tonic side. A Mixolydian ♭9, ♯9, ♭13 chord (also called an "altered dominant" or V7alt) is built on the fifth scale degree and contains:

G — B — D♭(♭9) — D#/E♭(♯9) — F — E♭(♭13)

The ♭9 and ♯9 simultaneously — the "jimi hendrix chord" voicing territory
Resolves to C (or Cm). The tension is almost unbearable. The resolution is ecstatic.

The ♯9 chord — the dominant 7th with a sharpened ninth — became the signature of 1960s funk and psychedelic rock (the "Purple Haze chord," the "Hendrix chord"). In R&B, the ♭9 adds an almost sinister gravity to the dominant, especially over minor resolutions. The ♭13 replaces the fifth and creates a wash of color that sits exactly between major and minor. Together, these alterations create what theorists call the altered scale — and what musicians call "that sound."

The connection to Spanish Phrygian is intimate: both are built on the same scale (the 7th mode of melodic minor / harmonic minor relationship). What flamenco hears as a tonic sound with a raised third, jazz hears as an altered dominant. Same pitches, different gravity. Context determines meaning.

Module VI

Bryan Adams: "Have You Really Ever Loved a Woman"
— It's Always the Canadians

Phrygian for Western Ears · The ♭II Chord · Michael Kamen · Flamenco Guitar in a Pop Song

There is a particular kind of beauty that only the Phrygian mode delivers to a Western ear — specifically a Western ear that does not already live in the flamenco tradition, the Middle Eastern tradition, or the Eastern European tradition where Phrygian is home territory. For that ear, Phrygian is exotic without being alien, ancient without being cold, tense without being threatening.

Bryan Adams' 1995 ballad from Don Juan DeMarco — co-written with Michael Kamen and Robert John "Mutt" Lange, orchestrated by Kamen — is perhaps the most perfectly calibrated deployment of Spanish Phrygian ever placed in a mainstream Western pop record. It is scored for nylon-string guitar, orchestra, and voice, and it opens with a guitar figure that is textbook Phrygian Dominant: the ♭II–I cadence in the key of E.

Phrygian Harmonic Movement — "Have You Really Ever Loved a Woman" Key center: E (E Phrygian Dominant / E Spanish Phrygian)

Recurring cadential figure: F major → E major
That F major chord is the ♭II — the Neapolitan — the defining gesture of Phrygian harmony.

The guitar line descends: E — D — C — B — then that F chord arrives
and resolves back to E with a sensation of inevitability,
as if the ♭II was always the only place the harmony could have gone.

What Kamen and Adams understood intuitively — and what makes this song a masterclass — is that the Western ear accepts Phrygian most readily in a romantic, cinematic context. The associations are already loaded: flamenco is passion, Spain is warmth, the nylon string guitar is sensual. The ♭II chord doesn't need to be explained; it is felt as the musical equivalent of a deep sigh, an ache, a longing that has no clean resolution because longing never does.

"The ♭II → I move doesn't resolve the tension. It is the tension. You arrive at the I chord not with relief but with a deeper, more beautiful ache."

Notice also how the melody over the ♭II chord (F major) sits on notes from the E Phrygian scale — specifically the ♭2 (F) and the ♭6 (C). These are the two notes that are most "non-Western" to a pop ear, and they are deployed at the melodic peak, the moment of maximum emotional exposure. This is not accidental. This is the craft.

The Canadians, it seems, have a particular gift for this. Celine Dion. Leonard Cohen. Joni Mitchell. And here, Bryan Adams and Mutt Lange — bringing a centuries-old Andalusian cadence to a film soundtrack and making it feel like the most natural expression of love in the world.

Ears-First Exercise

Before any theory: just listen to the opening 30 seconds. Then play F major on a piano or guitar. Then play E major. Just those two chords. Feel the motion. That is 500 years of flamenco compressed into two chords. Now you understand why it works.

Module VII

Tchaikovsky's Finale:
A First Chord That Is vii∅7.
I Shit You Not.

Pathétique Symphony · Half-Diminished · vii∅7 · When the Most Unstable Chord Opens Everything

Peter Ilyich Tchaikovsky's Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74 — the Pathétique, 1893, his last symphony, premiered nine days before his death — ends with a fourth movement marked Adagio lamentoso. It is one of the most devastating pieces of music ever written: a slow, inexorable descent into resignation, ending not with a dramatic flourish but with a quiet, fading B minor chord that simply stops. Many have heard a death wish in it.

But we are concerned here with the finale's opening gesture.

The fourth movement opens in the strings with what should, by every convention of 1893 orchestral writing, be a chord of tonic stability — a B minor chord, or perhaps a dominant F# — something to orient the listener. Tchaikovsky does neither.

The Opening Chord: vii∅7 in B minor

The chord that opens the fourth movement of the Pathétique is built on A# (the leading tone of B minor): A# — C# — E — G#.

This is the half-diminished seventh chord on the seventh scale degree — written as vii∅7 in Roman numeral analysis. In B minor, this means a chord built on A# with a diminished fifth, a minor third, and a minor seventh above it.

It is, by definition, the most unstable, forward-leaning chord in the diatonic system. It contains a tritone (A#–E). It has no stable fifth above the root. It desperately wants to resolve to the tonic. Tchaikovsky opens his final statement — his musical will and testament — with this chord.

What does it mean to begin with the most unresolved chord in the system? It means the music begins mid-tension, mid-grief, mid-longing — as if the movement has been in progress before we arrived, as if the pain existed before the first note sounded. There is no setup, no comfortable introduction. Tchaikovsky says: we are already there. We are already in the grief.

Recall from Module II: the vii∅7 (half-diminished) is the seventh chord built on the seventh degree of the major scale — and in minor keys, on the raised seventh degree (leading tone). It appears naturally in both major and harmonic minor. In jazz, it is the "ii∅7" in a minor ii–V–i progression (e.g., A∅7 → D7alt → Gm). In Spanish Phrygian contexts, it can act as the chord built on the lowered second degree.

But here, in Tchaikovsky, it is something more primal: it is the chord of unresolved longing given a body and made to speak first.

"Every other composer in 1893 would have opened the finale with the tonic or the dominant. Tchaikovsky opened it with the leading-tone half-diminished. He was telling you, from the first breath: this will not resolve the way you expect."

The half-diminished seventh chord is the via negativa of harmony: it is defined entirely by what it lacks (a stable fifth, a consonant root) and what it wants (to move to the dominant, to resolve to the tonic). As a first chord, it is a confession: I am not at rest. I have never been at rest. This music begins in need.

Tchaikovsky's use of the vii∅7 as an opening gesture is, in one sense, the ultimate application of everything in this curriculum: the formula that gives us 88 equal notes, the seventh chords we practice every morning, the melody sitting on the seventh in Stevie's voice, the borrowed darkness of modal interchange, the Phrygian ache of Bryan Adams' guitar — it all converges here, in an old Russian man writing his last symphony and opening the final movement with the most harmonically incomplete chord available to him. And making it the most honest note he ever played.

All Ages

Listen to the opening of the 4th movement. Say nothing. Feel it. Then — when you're ready — play a B diminished 7th on the piano and listen to where it wants to go.

Final Synthesis: The Whole Curriculum in One Chord

A440 gave us the equal-tempered system (Module I). The diatonic 7th chords gave us the vii∅7 as a household sound (Module II). Stevie showed us the 7th can be a place of rest rather than tension (Module III). Modal interchange showed us darkness can coexist with a major key (Module IV). Spanish Phrygian showed us the ♭II's longing (Module V–VI). And Tchaikovsky — at the end of his life, at the end of tonal Romanticism — showed us that the most unstable chord in the system, the one that has no home, the one that exists only in relation to where it's going, can be the first thing said. It can be the truest thing said.

Go practice your seventh chords. In all twelve keys. Every day. Until you understand why he did it.
